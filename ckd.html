About CKD

Chronic kidney disease, explained

Everything on this page applies whether you arrived here from the hypertension track or the diabetes track — the two conditions are the leading causes of CKD, and the kidney itself doesn't care which one got there first.

What is CKD?

Chronic kidney disease is a gradual, long-term loss of kidney function. Unlike acute kidney injury — a sudden drop caused by dehydration, blood loss or a heart attack, which can fully recover if the underlying cause is corrected — chronic damage builds up slowly, over years, and is usually not reversible.

What do the kidneys do?

The kidneys have three core jobs:

  • Maintaining the internal environment — the pH, water content and electrolyte balance of the body, so every cell and organ can function properly. This is why kidney disease often shows up as symptoms in other organs.
  • Excreting waste — removing the waste products of metabolism and of drugs from the body.
  • Producing hormones — for bone growth (vitamin D activation), blood production in the marrow (erythropoietin), and blood pressure regulation (renin).

What causes CKD?

The most important cause of chronic kidney disease is diabetes in the middle-aged or elderly population. High blood pressure is the other major cause, and the two conditions together produce worse outcomes than either alone. Both work the same way: they slowly damage the small blood vessels inside the kidney over years. A smaller share of cases are hereditary, such as polycystic kidney disease.

Why is CKD without symptoms?

The kidney has enormous reserve capacity. The body can adapt until as much as 90 percent of kidney function is damaged, which is why most patients feel nothing until very late. Detection depends on laboratory testing of blood and urine, not on how a person feels.

Albumin leak is the earliest warning sign. It shows up in a urine test long before blood urea or creatinine begin to rise, which only happen once kidney failure is already significant.

The structure of the kidney

Each kidney is a bean-shaped organ, roughly the size of a fist, positioned toward the back on either side of the spine, just below the rib cage. Inside, millions of microscopic filtering units called nephrons do the actual work of cleaning the blood and producing urine.

How can you detect CKD early?

Because there are no early symptoms, detection relies entirely on scheduled testing rather than waiting to feel unwell:

  • A urine test for albumin — the earliest detectable marker
  • A blood test for urea and creatinine — indicating how far the disease has progressed
  • An annual blood pressure check
  • An annual blood sugar check, especially if diabetic or hypertensive

Once you detect CKD, what can you do?

Early-stage CKD, caught at the albumin-leak stage, can often be slowed significantly through tight control of blood pressure and blood sugar, dietary changes, and avoiding drugs that are toxic to the kidney — particularly long-term, non-prescribed painkillers. The earlier it's caught, the more options remain.

Diet in CKD

Diet plays an important role once kidney disease is present. Because the kidneys manage the body's water, salt, potassium and protein balance, a diagnosis changes how each of these should be handled — which is precisely why a generic "eat healthy" approach isn't enough once CKD is confirmed, and why guidance should be tailored to kidney function specifically rather than general nutrition advice.
