Vitamin D deficiency

Vitamin D testing has become extremely common — and the majority of people tested turn out to show "low" levels. But is that really a deficiency?

It seems paradoxical: a country with abundant sunshine, widely found to be "deficient" in a vitamin the skin makes from sunlight. The real question is how the commonly available test should be interpreted.

How the test works

Vitamin D (cholecalciferol) forms in the skin from sunlight, then is converted in the liver to 25-hydroxycholecalciferol. The standard blood test measures the total amount of this compound, which is made up of three parts: roughly 85% bound to a vitamin D binding protein, about 15% bound to albumin, and a tiny free or bioavailable fraction of around 0.03%.

It's that free fraction that actually matters for bone health and vitamin D action — the protein-bound portion is more of a genetically-determined storage reserve.

What the research shows

In African Americans, the protein-bound form is genetically lower, reflecting generations of high sun exposure — yet the free, biologically active form is normal, and a Harvard Medical School study found they in fact had stronger bones despite lower total levels than white Americans. A similar genetic background, shaped by abundant regional sunlight, is plausible in India too — meaning the standard total-level test may not reflect a true deficiency here either.

Low vitamin D levels are also seen alongside many chronic conditions — heart attack, diabetes, hypertension. In these cases it appears to be a marker of the chronic disease, not its cause, and supplementation has not been shown to help.

The test is being run widely, and it's costly. It deserves careful interpretation to avoid unnecessary supplementation and its consequences.

References

  • Vitamin D binding protein and vitamin D status of Black Americans and White Americans. N Engl J Med 2013; 369:1991–2000.
  • The Lancet Diabetes & Endocrinology, Vol. II, Issue 1, pages 76–89, Jan 2014.
