Urinary tract infection

A common problem — about half of women will experience one in their lifetime — and one surrounded by more misconceptions than most.

Urinary tract infection is colonisation of the urinary tract by organisms. The tract runs from the collecting system in the kidney, through the ureter, to the bladder and urethra. The kidney and ureter make up the "upper" tract; the bladder and urethra make up the "lower" tract. It's normally sterile except at its very end — infection happens when organisms travel upward.

It's far more common in women, due to a shorter urethra and irritation that can occur during sexual activity. In men, it typically appears either in the first year of life (due to birth abnormalities) or after 60 (when an enlarged prostate blocks flow); outside of those windows, it is largely a disease of sexually active women.

Causes

Infections can be community-acquired (typically bacterial, most often E. coli) or hospital-acquired via catheterisation (which can involve resistant bacteria and fungi).

Symptoms

The classic symptoms are painful urination (dysuria) and increased frequency, caused by irritation of the bladder and urethra. Fever and loin pain point to an upper tract infection; lower abdominal pain and frequency alone point to a lower tract infection. Blood in the urine without dysuria does not, on its own, indicate infection.

If an organism grows in a urine culture with no symptoms at all, it's called asymptomatic bacteriuria — a lab finding that usually does not require treatment.

Diagnosis & interpreting reports

Diagnosis is mainly clinical: a sudden onset of dysuria with increased frequency is close to diagnostic on its own. Lab tests confirm and guide treatment. In a urine report, pus cells indicate inflammation (usually infection, but also possibly stones or tumours); albumin indicates kidney disease rather than uncomplicated infection; epithelial cells usually indicate contamination during collection. A colony count above 1 lakh (100,000) on culture is considered significant — be cautious of any culture report given without one.

Treatment & prevention

Simple, community-acquired lower tract infections can often be treated with single-dose antibiotics; others need a longer course. For sexually active women with recurrent infections, prevention centres on good hygiene, urinating after intercourse, and sometimes a low-dose preventive antibiotic. Drinking plenty of water reduces irritation (though it can dilute antibiotic concentration), and a vegetable-forward diet makes urine less acidic and less irritating than a high animal-protein diet.
