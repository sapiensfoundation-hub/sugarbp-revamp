Myths & facts about kidney disease

Kidney disease is one of the most misunderstood conditions in general conversation. A few corrections, drawn directly from clinical practice.

MythKidney disease is hereditary.
FactVery few kidney diseases are hereditary — polycystic kidney disease is one of the rare examples.
MythYou can have single or double kidney failure.
Fact"Kidney failure" refers to both kidneys not functioning. If only one kidney is affected, blood urea will not rise.
MythOnce dialysis starts, it has to be permanent.
FactIt depends on whether the kidney failure is acute or chronic. Acute failure may need only temporary dialysis.
MythIt isn't safe to donate a kidney.
FactIf a donor's general health is normal — no diabetes or high blood pressure — donation is safe. Donors go on to live normal lives, including marriage and childbirth.
MythKidney patients should drink large quantities of water.
FactWater often needs to be restricted, since maintaining water balance is one of the kidneys' own jobs, and impaired kidneys can't manage excess.
MythDrinking beer is good for the kidneys.
FactBeer's high water content simply increases urine output — it doesn't improve kidney function.
MythSalt substitutes are safe for kidney patients.
FactSalt substitutes are potassium chlorides, which are more dangerous in kidney failure since potassium excretion is already impaired.

Why kidney disease is on the rise

Detection has improved, but the underlying occurrence has also genuinely increased — driven by rising rates of diabetes and hypertension, lower physical activity, and wider use of drugs that are toxic to the kidney.

Acute vs. chronic

Acute kidney disease is a sudden drop in function from reduced blood supply — following dehydration, gastroenteritis, a heart attack, or blood loss. If the underlying cause is corrected quickly, the patient can recover fully. Chronic kidney disease develops slowly, most often from diabetes or hypertension, and is usually not reversible.

The body can adapt until as much as ninety percent of kidney function is damaged, which is why patients so often show no symptoms until very late. Albumin leak in the urine is the earliest warning sign; rising urea and creatinine in the blood only appear once the disease has become more severe.
