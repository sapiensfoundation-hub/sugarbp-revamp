Hydration

How much water should you drink?

It's not necessary to measure and consume a fixed amount of water. Intake can vary widely depending on thirst and cultural habit — and the body is very good at managing the rest.

About two-thirds of the body is composed of water, which moves freely between cells and the fluid surrounding them depending on osmotic activity. Water enters the body through what you drink and through the water generated by metabolising food, and leaves through sweat, urine and stool.

How the body regulates it

This balance is delicately managed by the brain and kidneys. An "osmostat" in the brain senses the concentration of the blood and triggers the antidiuretic hormone (ADH), which both stimulates thirst and reduces water loss in urine. As a result, the body's water composition stays remarkably constant even when intake varies a lot day to day.

The kidneys can concentrate urine down to as little as 500 ml a day, or dilute it up to 10 litres a day. A person with free access to water and a normally functioning brain essentially cannot become dehydrated — which is why animals never calculate their intake, only follow thirst, and live healthily on it.

When it changes

The kidney's ability to clear excess water — called free water clearance — can be affected by kidney disease, liver disease, heart disease, and certain drugs called diuretics. In these situations the body may retain extra water, which shows up as a drop in serum sodium, a condition called hyponatremia (serum sodium below 135 meq/l). This can be serious.

A healthy person doesn't need to measure water intake — thirst is an excellent guide. But in kidney disease, liver disease, heart disease, or while on diuretics, water may need to be actively restricted. Follow your clinician's guidance rather than a generic daily target.
